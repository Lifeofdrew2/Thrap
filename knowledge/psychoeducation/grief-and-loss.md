## Grief is not a schedule

When someone close to you has died, grief does not move through fixed stages on a
timetable, and people who expect it to often conclude they are doing it wrong. It
commonly arrives in waves, triggered by ordinary things, and can feel further away one
week and closer the next. Feeling relatively steady is not disloyalty, and a sudden
return of intensity is not a relapse.

## The range of what people feel

Alongside sadness people report numbness, anger, guilt, relief, anxiety about their own
health, and difficulty concentrating or remembering. Physical effects such as fatigue,
appetite change, and disturbed sleep are common. People are often unsettled by feelings
they judge to be inappropriate; the range is wider than most people expect.

## Losses other than bereavement

The same responses follow other losses: the end of a relationship, a miscarriage, a
serious diagnosis, losing a role or a sense of professional identity, migration, or
estrangement from family. These losses are frequently unacknowledged by others, which
can leave people isolated with them.

## Practical arrangements after a death

The period immediately after someone has died is often taken up with practical
matters: registering the death, arranging a funeral or burial, and handling the
affairs of the person who passed away. People frequently describe this stretch as
strangely busy, and find that grief arrives properly only once it is over.

## Returning to work

People often return to work before they feel ready, and find ordinary tasks
unexpectedly hard. Concentration is commonly affected for some time. Adjustments,
compassionate leave, and phased return are covered by `[APPROVED LEAVE POLICY]` and can
be discussed with `[NAMED HUMAN ROLE]`.

## When support is likely to help

Counselling can help when grief following a death or another loss is not easing over
time, when a person cannot function day to day, when the loss was sudden or traumatic,
when guilt or anger dominates, or simply when a person wants somewhere to speak about
it without managing anyone else's feelings.

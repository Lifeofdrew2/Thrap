## Why it takes a toll

Sustained conflict with a manager or colleague is one of the most common reasons people
use workplace counselling. It occupies attention outside working hours, disturbs sleep,
and often becomes the lens through which a person judges their own competence.

## Separating the two routes

Two different things are usually needed, and confusing them leaves people stuck.

Counselling supports the person: the stress, the loss of confidence, and how they want
to handle the situation. It cannot change another person's behaviour or make a finding
about what happened.

The workplace route addresses the situation itself: raising a concern, mediation, or a
formal process. That route is `[APPROVED WORKPLACE ROUTE]` and is owned by
`[NAMED HUMAN ROLE]`.

Using one does not prevent using the other, and using counselling is not a substitute
for reporting behaviour that should be reported.

## Bullying, harassment, and discrimination

Where behaviour may amount to bullying, harassment, or discrimination, it is covered by
`[APPROVED POLICY]` and should be raised through `[APPROVED REPORTING ROUTE]`. A person
does not have to be certain that behaviour meets a definition before raising it.

This navigation service does not investigate, judge whether a policy has been breached,
or record allegations.

## Managers seeking support

A manager may use the programme for their own support, and may separately ask
`[NAMED HUMAN ROLE]` for guidance on supporting a team member. A manager cannot refer an
employee into counselling on their behalf or receive information about whether an
employee used the service.

## Concern about a colleague

Where someone is worried about a colleague, the right step is to encourage that person
to contact the service themselves, and to raise a safety concern with
`[NAMED HUMAN ROLE]` where there is any risk to wellbeing. Concerns about a named third
party are handled by a human role, not by this service.

## What people describe

Low mood is often described as flatness rather than sadness — going through the motions,
finding no pleasure in things that used to matter, or feeling heavy and slowed down.
Some people describe irritability or numbness instead of sadness, and are surprised when
that turns out to be part of the same picture.

## Common features

People commonly notice changes in sleep in either direction, appetite changes, low
energy, difficulty concentrating or deciding, withdrawing from other people, and a harsh
inner commentary about themselves. Mornings are often the hardest part of the day.

## Why it is easy to dismiss

Low mood frequently arrives gradually, which makes it easy to treat as a personality
trait or an unavoidable consequence of circumstances. People often say they did not
think it was "bad enough" to mention. Persistent low mood responds to support, and
waiting for it to become severe is not a requirement for asking.

## When it is affecting work

Low mood can show up at work as missed deadlines, slower output, avoiding colleagues, or
uncharacteristic mistakes. People often spend considerable energy hiding it, which is
itself exhausting. The programme is confidential and separate from performance
management.

## Signs that professional support would help

Support is likely to help when low mood has lasted more than two weeks, when it is
affecting sleep, appetite, work, or relationships, when a person feels hopeless about
things changing, or when they feel alone with it.

This service does not diagnose depression, assess severity, or discuss medication. Those
belong with a qualified clinician.
